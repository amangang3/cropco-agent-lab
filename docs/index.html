The situation

Which farms do we go after?

You work in commercial operations at Bayer Crop Science. Bayer sells to roughly 10,000 of the 40,000 big row-crop farms in the country. Nobody is going to fund four times the sales force to reach the rest, so the question is which farms to pick — and who decides.

What you have

Ten farms. A CRM row for each, plus call notes from four different reps over eighteen months. The notes are uneven. Some contradict the CRM. One farm has almost nothing. Real files look like this.

What the job is

Profile each farm, score it against the rubric, rank them, and write outreach to the top five. If you can't judge a farm honestly, don't guess — set it aside for a person.

The next thirty minutes

  1. 8 min You do it, in your own chatbot
  2. 4 min Meet the agent team
  3. 5 min Watch them do the same task
  4. 6 min Read what they produced
  5. 7 min Talk about the difference

You won't finish part one, and you're not meant to. How far you get in eight minutes is the number everything else gets compared against. Work the farms in order and don't skip ahead.

Part 2 · 4 minutes · together

        
The team you're handing it to

        

          Three agents. There's no code behind them — each one is a page of plain
          English saying what it does, what it's allowed to read, and when to
          stop.
        

      


      

        

          
1

          
farm-researcher

          
Reads · doesn't judge

          

            Turns a CRM row and a rep's notes into a profile. Its main job is to
            flag problems instead of tidying them away — a number
            that contradicts the CRM, missing notes, nobody who can sign, a
            customer who asked not to be contacted.
          

          
Two copies run at once, five farms each.

        


        

          
2

          
fit-scorer

          
Scores · and refuses to

          

            Applies the rubric, and nothing but the rubric. It also carries the
            rule this whole exercise turns on: a flagged farm doesn't get
            a score at all. Not a low score — no score, with a written
            reason, even if it would have come first.
          

          
Can't start until all ten profiles exist.

        


        

          
3

          
outreach-writer

          
Writes · to five people

          

            Drafts the outreach. It gets the ranked list and the
            review list, so it knows who not to write to as well
            as who to write to. 120 words, one specific detail, one small ask.
          

          
Runs last, on the top five only.

        

      


      

      
    
        
  • Nobody is in charge. One session starts each agent in the order a person specified, and waits when it's told to wait. It doesn't judge any farm itself.
    • 
        
  • The order isn't arbitrary. Research can happen in parallel because the farms don't depend on each other. Scoring can't start early, because a ranking built on half the file would look complete and be wrong.
    • 
      


      

        
So where does a person come in?

        

          The run you're about to watch takes three and a half minutes and nobody
          touches it. It's easy to conclude there's no human in this at all.
          There are three, and they matter more than the agents do.
        

      


      

      
    
        
  • The judgement is in the writing, not the model. In a few minutes you'll watch it throw out the biggest account in the file. It doesn't work that out. It follows a rule somebody typed.
    • 
        
  • Nothing here reaches a customer. The run ends at a draft. Deciding to send one is a separate act, by a person, and the team is built on the assumption that it will be.
    • 
      


      

        Go deeper — read the actual instruction files
        

          

            The three files, unedited. The rule that matters is in
            fit-scorer, under “Confidence, and when to stop scoring”.

Part 4 · 6 minutes · in pairs

        
Read it like it landed in your inbox

        

          Three files came out. Turn to the person next to you and work these
          three questions. The reading is the exercise — the speed was the least
          interesting thing you just watched.
        

      


      

        

          
Q1

          
Would you send those five drafts as they're written?

        

        

          
Q2

          

            Four farms got handed back. Which one would have done the most damage
            if it had quietly been scored and mailed instead?
          

        

        

          
Q3

          

            Why did it stop on that farm? Not how it knew —
            where is the sentence that made it stop, and who would have had
            to write it?
          

        

      


      

        

        

      


      

        

          Read the end of outreach-drafts.md.
          Under “Before any of these are sent”, the writer lists what it can't
          vouch for: names it took from call notes, a data package it isn't sure
          exists, a meeting it committed someone to. It flagged those itself and
          then stopped. Somebody still has to go and check them.
